MyVR · Research act 02

Understand the market.
Design the opening.

The location-based entertainment market is not one business model. Some suppliers sell a complete attraction, some license games, some manage devices and content, and some support installers who serve multiple venues. The opportunity is to understand those layers before deciding where a Workshop-led distributor model belongs.

Research snapshot: 31 October 2026. This page summarizes public vendor material, not independent audits. Prices and capabilities can change; vendor-reported venue counts, outcomes, and product claims are not independently verified here.

01 · The market has several layers

Different suppliers solve different problems.

Turnkey attraction

Sell the complete arena system

Hardware, software, content, onboarding, support, and an operating portal are packaged together. This reduces integration work for the venue, but creates a larger initial commitment and an ongoing supplier relationship.

Content subscription

Sell access to a game catalogue

The operator pays a fixed recurring amount for content and support. The venue may retain more freedom over its own ticket pricing and business operations, while content access remains tied to the subscription.

Content marketplace + management

Separate distribution from the venue

A platform can license content, launch software, manage stations, and provide creator distribution. This creates a marketplace relationship between creators, platform, and operators.

Installer / channel partner

Let a local partner serve many venues

Installers can deploy systems, manage multiple arenas, and provide first-line customer support. The platform provider supplies the underlying tooling and licenses.

Traditional laser tag

Extend an installed equipment base

Established vendors monetize equipment, licenses, support, updates, consumables, and sometimes per-player usage. This matters because a VR layer may need to complement—not replace—the venue's current system.

In-house / commissioned

Build or buy bespoke experiences

A venue can hire developers or studios for custom content. This may fit a unique attraction, but the operator must clarify who owns the work, maintains it, and funds future changes.

03 · Strategic implications

Where the distributor hypothesis could fit.

The research does not show that the market lacks platforms or multi-site management. It shows that these capabilities are often bundled with a specific vendor's content, hardware, or brand. The potential opening is a partner-owned route to market built on a reusable, technology-neutral Workshop ecosystem—with clear separation between platform stewardship and local customer ownership.

1. Partner owns the local relationship

The anchor distributor could sell to venues, coordinate installations, train staff, and provide first-line support. The Workshop should not pretend it can scale local service without a partner network.

2. Each partner gets a controlled domain

A branded domain and distributor console are proposed capabilities. They would need tenant isolation, scoped permissions, audit trails, billing attribution, deployment approval, and clean offboarding.

3. Venues retain operating autonomy

Venue owners should control their local schedules, staff, pricing, safety procedures, and customer operations. The distributor manages deployment relationships, not the venue's entire business.

4. Workshop earns a modest platform return

A small share may be possible if revenue is recurring and support is efficiently delivered. It is not proven until volume, collection costs, hosting, support, insurance, and partner economics are modeled.

Questions the next research phase must answer

  • Can the proposed experience layer coexist with the arena's current laser-tag equipment and operating workflow?
  • Who owns venue data, player records, experience builds, and customer billing relationships?
  • Can a distributor onboard and support multiple venues without receiving access to unrelated partners or Workshop infrastructure?
  • What minimum monthly contribution pays for hosting, support, maintenance, and continued development?
  • What happens to a venue's data, configuration, and purchased equipment if a distributor relationship ends?
  • Which experience formats actually create repeat bookings rather than simply adding novelty?

The Workshop's self-built technology may reduce dependency on expensive proprietary runtime libraries. It does not remove operating costs or establish that the system is ready for commercial deployment. Repository licenses, hardware support, security, safety, and service obligations still require validation.

Working conclusion: compete on partner enablement, reusable experiences, transparent economics, and controlled multi-venue distribution—not on an unsupported claim that no one else offers arena management or VR content distribution.
